Collaboration: explain, listen, paraphrase, question, and revise

  1. Independent start: each learner marks the endpoints and writes an initial equation. Partners must have something of their own to discuss.
  2. Explainer: point to each quantity and explain what the equation represents. State the direction of change and a check. Do not read a final number only.
  3. Listener: first paraphrase the relationship: “You started at __, ended at __, and found __.” The explainer confirms or corrects the paraphrase.
  4. Questioner: ask “What does the sign of your answer mean?” or “Which number is the initial value in your equation?” Choose a question that tests the actual explanation, not a scripted compliment.
  5. Swap: the second learner explains the reverse-change card; the first paraphrases and questions. Both roles include mathematical reasoning.
  6. Individual revision: each learner writes what changed or why their reasoning still holds, and later solves a new task alone.

For triads, add an evidence-checker who matches a spoken claim to the diagram; rotate that role as well. Do not make a fluent speaker permanently explain for another student. Invite rehearsal in a language or communication mode that supports access when appropriate, then build the target disciplinary explanation. Record who contributed which reasoning and what support was used.

Blank student materials: temperature task and equation cards

Task sheet: What changed? These are original hypothetical measurement values for mathematical practice, not classroom results or claims about actual weather.

TimeTemperature
7:00 a.m.−1.5°C
Noon.75°C
  1. Identify the initial value, final value, and unknown. What does “change from 7:00 a.m. to noon” ask you to find?
  2. Show the initial and final temperatures on a number line with equal intervals. Draw a directed arrow from the initial to the final value. Label the arrow with the change.
  3. Write an equation for the relationship, find the change, and explain the meaning of its sign and unit.
  4. Write an equivalent subtraction and addition-of-the-opposite expression. Explain why they give the same change.
  5. What is the distance between the temperatures on the number line? Explain how distance is related to, but different from, directed change.
  6. Check your result using initial + change = final. Describe whether the check confirms your answer or leads you to revise it.

Language reference: initial means starting; final means ending; change is the directed amount connecting them; distance is nonnegative separation; an opposite/additive inverse combines with the original number to make zero. Use these meanings to explain a relationship, not as a list to copy.

Number-line and language supports to prepare

Prepare two horizontal strips with equal quarter-unit intervals from −3 to 3. One is labeled positions and directed change; the other is labeled calculation of the difference. Clearly label 0 and the whole numbers; show quarter-unit ticks. Provide movable arrows or counters, an enlarged high-contrast version, and tactile markers where appropriate. A vertical thermometer-shaped line may provide an additional representation if learners connect it explicitly to the horizontal line.

Word and symbol meanings: initial = starting value; final = ending value; change = the directed amount connecting them; distance = nonnegative separation; opposite/additive inverse = a number that combines with the original to make zero. In .75 − (−1.5), the first minus is a subtraction operation and the second identifies a negative number. Teach this in context, not as an isolated vocabulary quiz.

Students may begin with arrows, gestures, words, or symbols. Require a connection among the representations before treating them as evidence of the same understanding. Physical movement is a way to make the relation inspectable, not a claim that a learner has a fixed kinesthetic style.

Blank individual transfer task and metacognitive check

Work independently after the discussion. A sensor reads −.25°C at one time and −1.5°C later. Find the change from the first reading to the later reading.

  1. Mark both readings on a number line. Draw and label the directed change.
  2. Write final − initial and an equivalent addition-of-the-opposite expression. Calculate the signed change and interpret it with a unit.
  3. Find the distance between the two readings. Explain why the distance and directed change need not have the same sign.
  4. Use initial + change = final to check your result. If you revise, keep the original work visible and explain the reason.
  5. Write a short explanation using clear initial/final referents and a mathematical reason. You may use a labeled diagram as evidence, but include your own connecting explanation.
  6. Optional transfer: show the change from −3/4 to 1/2, then the reverse change. Explain what stays the same and what changes.

Reflection: I first expected __ because __. I checked __. The check confirmed/changed __. A question I still have is __. These blanks are collection prompts, not student responses. Preserve required accommodations and record any assistance.

Separate the mathematics from the language—and both from participation

ALD L2: interpretive/collaborative languageAccurately paraphrases a partner’s initial/final/change relationship and asks a relevant question or comparison.Nodding, taking a turn, repeating the answer, or correcting without first understanding the claim.
Metacognitive informationIdentifies and carries out a meaningful check, then explains a revision or confirmation.“I checked” without an inspectable action or link to the work.

For this proposed lesson, meeting the content goal requires C1–C3 on the new individual task; meeting the ALD goal requires attributable explanatory and collaborative evidence, not a whole-class impression. Record missing evidence as missing. Content understanding may be visible in a diagram despite an incomplete English explanation; that is a reason to distinguish the judgments and plan language support, not to erase either need.
