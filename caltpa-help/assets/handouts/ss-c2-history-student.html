Learner packet: source set and inquiry tools

Teacher-written context: Industrialization changed how labor was organized and intensified public debate about working conditions. In Britain, the 1833 Factory Act restricted some child factory labor and established an inspectorate. A law states what is required; it does not by itself show compliance. This lesson examines what a later inspector’s account can and cannot establish. It does not ask students to role-play exploited children, defend abuse or disclose family work experiences. Discuss historical evidence with care and allow a private way to ask for support.

Source C: Keating-Owen Child Labor Act, U.S. Congress, approved September 1, 1916, section 3 (excerpt). The full act sought to restrict interstate and foreign shipment of goods produced under specified child-labor conditions. Read the enforcement language below; do not treat it as a report that inspections occurred.

That for the purpose of securing proper enforcement of this Act the Secretary of Labor, or any person duly authorized by him, shall have authority to enter and inspect at any time mines quarries, mills, canneries, workshops, factories, manufacturing establishments, and other places in which goods are produced or held for interstate commerce; and the Secretary of Labor shall have authority to employ such assistance for the purposes of this Act as may from time to time be authorized by appropriation or other law.

Source D: modern archival context, teacher paraphrase. The U.S. National Archives explains that the Supreme Court invalidated this act in Hammer v. Dagenhart in 1918. That later event matters when evaluating a claim that passing the 1916 act permanently ended child labor. This contextual statement is from a modern secondary account, not part of the quoted statute. The 1916 law is historical, not current legal guidance.

Word and sentence study: explicitly teach regulate → regulation, enforce → enforcement and inspect → inspection/inspector. Show how the suffix changes a verb into a process or person noun. Read and use the whole word in a source-linked sentence. Unpack the long statutory sentence by finding the actor, authority granted and places covered; do not guess its meaning from one morpheme. Keep the original and a clearly labeled teacher paraphrase side by side.

A protocol for genuine disciplinary collaboration

Collaboration is an intellectual routine, not simply seating students together. Each student first makes a private attempt. In pairs, the explainer states a claim and points to evidence; the listener paraphrases it before asking an evidence question. The original speaker confirms or corrects the paraphrase. Partners switch roles, negotiate a revision, and record what changed and why. Everyone then completes a new individual response. Do not assign the same student permanently to note-taking or translating; both students need opportunities to read, speak, listen, reason and write.

Observable collaboration—not a participation tally
MoveWhat a teacher can look for
PrepareA dated individual prediction, annotation or model before the exchange.
Listen accuratelyThe listener restates the claim and evidence in a way the speaker accepts or corrects.
Challenge respectfullyThe partner asks what a line, number or arrow supports and what it does not establish.
Revise with a reasonThe learner names the evidence that changed or strengthened the reasoning. Agreement is not required.
Transfer independentlyA new response shows what the individual can do after support; a strong pair product alone is insufficient.

The language target is a disciplinary action, not a vocabulary count. Teach how to link a claim to evidence and qualify a conclusion. Model one short example, unpack the language, give guided practice, then remove unnecessary scaffolds. Let students draw, gesture, annotate and rehearse using their language resources before producing the required English response. Select the appropriate Emerging, Expanding or Bridging descriptors using actual language evidence; an ELPAC level alone cannot tell you how a student handles this specific task. Do not turn the conference into a debate over whether children deserved protection. The evidence question is what the documents allow historians to conclude. Partners can disagree about an inference while respecting the people whose experiences appear in the records. A learner can choose not to share personal experience and still fully participate.

Independent summative task—student copy

Student task: A museum draft says, “These records prove that governments ended industrial child labor once they passed laws.” Write a 200–300 word evidence-based replacement, or an agreed concise equivalent that preserves the same reasoning demand. Use Source B and Source C, with Source D for later context. State a qualified claim, use at least two precise details, explain what each contributes, address an alternative reading of the enforcement record, and identify one missing kind of evidence. Give dates/countries so the reader cannot mistake the sources for one before/after study. Include a short source list.

Conditional re-teach: a new historical detail

Use only if actual focus-student evidence identifies a rule/action/outcome confusion. Read a previously unused entry from the same archived report. The 1 October 1862 row names John Howell, Mynyddislwyn, records four young persons employed after 2 p.m. on Saturday, enters a £1 penalty and notes three cases withdrawn on payment of costs. This is a teacher-organized, shortened selection of an actual row—not invented testimony. Compare it with section 3 of the 1916 U.S. act already read. Which source grants authority, and which records a case? Write an attributed contrast and explain why neither alone proves that all factories complied. Identify one further record you would seek.

Conditional extension: choose evidence that can test a claim

Use only if actual evidence supports extension. A historian wants to test the claim “Enforcement became more consistent in this British region over time.” Three proposed search targets are available: (A) another printed copy of the same law, (B) a regional inspection register with dated visits, including visits where no offence was found, across several years, or (C) a later reformer’s opinion article. These are proposed search targets, not documents claimed to have been found. Which would most directly help test the claim, and why? Explain what denominator and contextual information you would still need. State one way another target could be useful for a different historical question. Discuss a partner’s challenge before writing your own qualified recommendation.
