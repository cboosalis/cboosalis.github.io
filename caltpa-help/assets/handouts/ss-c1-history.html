What this example teaches

Proposed teaching design—not a completed CalTPA submission. Tasks, teacher keys, likely interpretation branches and recording plans are examples. No class size, learner profile, assessment result, teaching video, score or learning outcome has been supplied. Replace planning assumptions with your actual placement evidence. The lesson minute ranges are proposed pacing, never video timestamps.

Historical reasoning is not deciding whether a source is simply “biased” or “unbiased.” Ask who created it, why, what the creator could know, what the text says and which conclusion the evidence can support. The inspector’s purpose matters without making every statement either automatically true or useless. A cautious, well-supported claim can be stronger than a confident sweeping judgment.

Standards, goals and criteria

Content goal: analyze a primary report and write a bounded claim supported by two details, identifying one limit. ALD goal: attribute the account, connect evidence to inference, and qualify scope while engaging in genuine partner listening.

Use actual prior evidence

Before this lesson, ask students to source a short document already studied: identify its creator/date, mark one stated detail, and distinguish it from an inference. Compare the written answer with a brief explanation. If a learner can explain the distinction orally but the written attribution is unclear, plan language support; if both modes treat one case as national proof, plan a source-scope model. Record those actual findings before selecting focus students or claiming a need. Do not assign a personal history because the lesson concerns labor.

Learner packet: context, original source and questions

Teacher-written context: Industrialization changed how labor was organized and intensified public debate about working conditions. In Britain, the 1833 Factory Act restricted some child factory labor and established an inspectorate. A law states what is required; it does not by itself show compliance. This lesson examines what a later inspector’s account can and cannot establish. It does not ask students to role-play exploited children, defend abuse or disclose family work experiences. Discuss historical evidence with care and allow a private way to ask for support.

UK National Archives: 1833 Factory Act background · CDE: HSS 10.3.4, work and labor in industrialization

Source A: factory inspector’s report, 1836. The National Archives identifies this extract as British Parliamentary Papers (1836), p. 353. Read the original text below before the modern paraphrase. Paragraph labels are added for classroom reference; they are not in the original.

A1. My Lord, in the case of Taylor, Ibbotson & Co. I took the evidence from the mouths of the boys themselves. They stated to me that they commenced working on Friday morning, the 27th of May last, at six A.M., and that, with the exception of meal hours and one hour at midnight extra, they did not cease working till four o’clock on Saturday evening, having been two days and a night thus engaged.

A2. Believing the case scarcely possible, I asked every boy the same questions, and from each received the same answers. I then went into the house to look at the time book, and in the presence of one of the masters, referred to the cruelty of the case, and stated that I should certainly punish it with all the severity in my power. Mr Rayner, the certificating surgeon of Bastile, was with me at the time.

Teacher-written access paraphrase—not another historical witness: The inspector says the boys reported an exceptionally long work period. He describes checking their accounts individually, going to consult a time book, and threatening punishment in a manager’s presence. The excerpt does not print the time book or the legal outcome. Use the paraphrase to check understanding; cite the original wording as evidence.

  1. Identify the kind of source, its date, the person speaking in the excerpt and the people whose accounts he reports. What does this chain of reporting mean for your use of the evidence?
  2. Mark two details describing the reported work period and the inspector’s attempt to investigate. Label reported testimony separately from the inspector’s own reported action.
  3. Calculate the elapsed time from Friday 6 a.m. to Saturday 4 p.m. Can you calculate the exact hours worked? Explain what information is missing.
  4. Evaluate this teacher-created claim: “This report proves the Factory Act changed nothing anywhere in Britain.” Which words exceed the source’s reach?
  5. Write a stronger claim with two details and one limit. Ask your partner to paraphrase your claim, challenge one inference, and name additional evidence that would help.
  6. Independently revise or defend your claim. Explain what you changed, or why the evidence justified keeping it.

Word study in context: teach inspect, inspection, inspector: the base carries the action and the suffix changes the word’s grammatical role. Read the whole words, locate them in a historical statement, and compose a sentence using the correct form. Explain unfamiliar historical terms such as “certificating surgeon” using an accurate glossary, not word-part guessing.

Collaboration changes the historical claim

Collaboration is an intellectual routine, not simply seating students together. Each student first makes a private attempt. In pairs, the explainer states a claim and points to evidence; the listener paraphrases it before asking an evidence question. The original speaker confirms or corrects the paraphrase. Partners switch roles, negotiate a revision, and record what changed and why. Everyone then completes a new individual response. Do not assign the same student permanently to note-taking or translating; both students need opportunities to read, speak, listen, reason and write.

Language that keeps historical claims honest

The language target is a disciplinary action, not a vocabulary count. Teach how to link a claim to evidence and qualify a conclusion. Model one short example, unpack the language, give guided practice, then remove unnecessary scaffolds. Let students draw, gesture, annotate and rehearse using their language resources before producing the required English response. Compare “The boys worked 34 hours” with “The inspector reports an elapsed span of 34 hours, with breaks not fully specified.” The second sentence is more precise because it distinguishes elapsed time, actual work time and source attribution. Reported is not a synonym for false; it identifies how the information reaches the reader. Help learners preserve that distinction in speech and writing.

Independent exit task—student copy

Student task: Write a 100–150 word museum label, or a concise equivalent agreed before assessment, answering: “What does this report show about industrial child labor and the difficulty of enforcing limits?” Include source/date, two details, a reasoned inference, one limit and the next source you would seek. Do not invent an inspection outcome. Finish with a separate note explaining which partner question helped you check your reasoning.

Teacher interpretation key—not student work

Teacher interpretation key—not a model of actual student achievement: The report describes boys’ accounts of work extending from Friday morning to Saturday afternoon, and the inspector’s stated effort to question them and consult a time book. The elapsed span is 34 hours; exact work hours cannot be computed because meal breaks are not quantified. It supports concern about this documented firm and evidence of attempted enforcement. It does not establish all factories’ conditions, a national before/after rate, the boys’ ages, or that punishment actually occurred. A useful next source might be the time book, court outcome, other inspections or evidence of workplaces that complied. Accept different justified claims within these limits.

Evidence and camera plan

Before teaching, follow your school and program’s consent procedures, identify who may appear, and store recordings only in approved locations. Test the actual device in the room: battery, storage, focus, lighting and whether both teacher and student voices are intelligible. Put a stable camera at a side/back angle that includes the teaching area and a consented discussion table without blocking movement. Avoid filming unconsented learners, displayed names, gradebooks or private records. A teacher-only microphone may lose the student thinking you need; test a permitted table microphone or a closer camera position during ordinary discussion.

Plan where students will work before the recording begins, but do not stage answers or rehearse an assessed performance for the camera. Retain the original securely. Later select authentic moments and follow the current guide’s editing and technical rules and log clip number, exact start/end, prompt, visible action, student response and limitation. A seat map does not replace audible evidence. Photograph or scan work separately when the camera cannot show writing legibly.

When something goes wrong, respond to the learning and then analyze it honestly. An incomplete explanation, disagreement or unsuccessful support can yield useful evidence if you describe what actually happened and why you changed course. Missing sound or an unseen learner cannot be repaired by inventing dialogue. Do not stage a reenactment; ask your program how to handle unusable required evidence. Planning a recording beat is not a guarantee that a usable clip exists.

Important recording beat: Save the initial and revised claim and capture both partner voices. Camera evidence of quiet compliance is not evidence of historical reasoning. If an emotional reaction or disagreement interrupts discussion, prioritize support and accurately explain what happened; do not use the lesson to solicit personal disclosures.
