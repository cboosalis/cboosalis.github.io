A collaboration routine that produces reasoning, listening, and individual evidence

  1. Think and mark independently. Before talk, each learner estimates the quotient and writes an initial reason. Keep this first attempt.
  2. Explainer: identify the claim, point to the representation or division step, and explain the relationship. Do not just announce a decimal.
  3. Listener–checker: paraphrase first: “Your claim is __; your evidence is __.” Ask one genuine clarification or challenge: “Why does that remainder show repetition?” or “How does the number line check the quotient?” Do not replace the explanation with your own.
  4. Confirm and swap. The explainer confirms or repairs the paraphrase; the partners change roles for a different example. Rotate roles so no learner becomes the permanent writer, translator, or helper.
  5. Revise individually. Each learner writes what changed or why the original reasoning still holds. A pair’s shared answer is not two independent demonstrations.
  6. Transfer independently. Use a new fraction without partner coaching; retain all required accommodations. Record the support conditions.

Teacher participation matters: listen long enough to identify the mathematical issue, ask for evidence, and leave the next reasoning move to students. Use a planned pause for silent thinking before inviting a contribution. Allow diagrams, gestures, home-language rehearsal, or access tools when appropriate, then explicitly connect those resources to the target explanation in English. Do not confuse these options with lowering the content goal.

Student Practice A: fraction–division connections

Original student tasks. Use your own paper for diagrams and explanations. Work alone first; keep the first attempt visible when you revise.

  1. A1. Before calculating, compare 3/4 and 4/3 with 1. Draw approximate locations on a 0–2 number line. Write a division expression for each. Explain why the expressions cannot be exchanged.
  2. A2. Three meters of ribbon are divided into four equal lengths. How long is each length? Show long division and check by reconstructing the 3 meters. What does each number in 3 ÷ 4 represent?
  3. A3. A teacher-created example claims 3/4 = .075. Use magnitude or multiplication to evaluate the claim. Write a correction and an explanation, not just a new answer.
  4. A4, independent exit. Convert 5/4 and −3/4 using long division. Show where each belongs relative to −1, 0, 1, and 2. Explain one check you used.
  5. Reflection. Before I calculated, I expected __. My check was __ because __. It confirmed or changed __.

Partner record: My partner’s claim was __. I paraphrased it as __. My question was __. My own revised or retained reason is __. These are blank prompts, not a scripted student conversation.

Student Practice B: read the remainder, not only the digits

  1. B1. Estimate 3/8, then use long division. Record the quotient digits and the successive remainder numbers. Why can the decimal end after the thousandths place?
  2. B2. Convert 1/6. After the first decimal digit, what remainder state returns? Explain what that means for later digits. Use repeating notation or write an ellipsis and explain its meaning.
  3. B3, reciprocal pair task. One partner investigates 7/8; the other investigates 2/3. Both estimate first, divide, and explain the remainder evidence. Paraphrase your partner’s reasoning and ask a question before agreeing or disagreeing.
  4. B4, independent exit. Convert 3/5 and 5/6 by long division. Explain why one terminates and the other repeats. For one answer, check the magnitude against 0, 1/2, and 1.
  5. Reflection. What would you now check before deciding that a decimal repeats? How did a remainder record help, or where is your reasoning still uncertain?

Student Practice C: when is a decimal exact?

  1. C1. Estimate and then convert 7/8 and 8/9. Which is greater? Use an exact comparison or a justified decimal comparison.
  2. C2. Evaluate the teacher-created statement “8/9 = .89 exactly because the display rounds to .89.” What part is useful? What part needs correction? Write a claim, division/remainder evidence, and a reason.
  3. C3. Make two true statements about 8/9: one using = and one using ≈. Explain what the approximation loses and when rounding to hundredths might be useful.
  4. C4. Before the final assessment, select one check you will use: magnitude, reconstruction by multiplication, remainder pattern, or notation. Explain when that check can detect an error and what it cannot establish by itself.

Blank summative assessment: individual fraction–decimal explanation

Student directions: Work independently. Show long division, not only a calculator result. Estimate or locate each number first. Use words, labeled notation, and a diagram as needed to make your reasoning clear. Write your own explanation; use the access tools and accommodations arranged by your teacher. A spelling error does not erase a clear mathematical reason.

  1. 1. Convert 5/8 to a decimal. Show the dividend/divisor setup and division. Explain how your remainder evidence shows whether the decimal terminates or repeats. Give a magnitude check.
  2. 2. Convert −7/4 to a decimal. Show the division, retain the sign, and explain where the number belongs relative to −2 and −1. Explain whether its decimal terminates or repeats.
  3. 3. Convert 2/3 to a decimal. Show enough long division to identify the pattern. Explain what repeats and why the division will continue that way. Represent the exact decimal with a repeating bar or clearly explained ellipsis.
  4. 4. Evaluate a claim. A teacher-created statement says, “2/3 = .67 exactly.” Decide whether the statement is mathematically correct. Write a clear claim, cite your division/remainder evidence, and explain your reason. Include a correct use of = or ≈.
  5. 5. Optional depth-of-reasoning item. For a fraction a/b with integers a and positive b, the possible remainder numbers are 0, 1, …, b−1. Use that fact to explain why its decimal must terminate or eventually repeat. This item can supply evidence beyond the unit’s core demonstrations; completing more items alone is not “exceeding.”
  6. Reflection, recorded separately. Name one check you actually used. Point to where it confirmed or changed your work. What would you check next if you had more time?

Performance criteria: judge content and ELD separately

ELD L1: clear mathematical claimAn intelligible statement addresses the question and makes referents clear: which fraction, decimal, or claim is being discussed.An isolated number or unclear “it/that” prevents the reader from identifying the claim.
ELD L2: relevant evidenceCites a specific division step, remainder state, signed magnitude, or equivalent relationship relevant to the claim.Lists vocabulary or unrelated calculations without showing their connection.
ELD L3: explanatory connectionConnects evidence to the conclusion in an understandable reason, using suitable mathematical terms and relational language.A claim and evidence are present but no intelligible reason links them. Accent, dialect, or unrelated spelling is not a separate deficit criterion.

Predetermine the overall categories: Content “met” requires C1–C3 across the core tasks; a partial profile is “not yet met” with the exact missing evidence recorded. Content “exceeded” requires the core evidence plus a correct general remainder argument or comparably deeper transfer, not speed or extra problems. ELD “met” requires L1–L3 in a coherent written justification; ELD “exceeded” adds an independently connected counterclaim or generalization with appropriately qualified reasoning. Decide how to handle incomplete/unscorable work before tallying; keep it visible rather than quietly counting it as assessed mastery.

Score the language organization and clarity separately from mathematical correctness. An articulate but mathematically false claim can meet some language criteria while failing content criteria; explain that pattern. Conversely, correct division with an unclear explanation does not prove the ELD goal. The criteria are classroom performance criteria, not CalTPA rubric levels or invented official scores. Review borderline cases consistently, retain the original work, and do not create demonstration class counts.

Blank follow-up task cards

Blank re-teaching card: (1) Estimate and convert 4/5 and 4/9 by long division. (2) Record the remainder states. (3) Evaluate “4/9 = .44 exactly.” (4) Write a claim, evidence, and reason. (5) Independently convert 1/3 and explain the relationship between 1/3 and .33. Name the check you used.

Blank extension card: Both 2/3 and 667/1000 round to .67 to the nearest hundredth. Are they the same number? Use exact reasoning. Explain why the rounded display is insufficient. Create and justify a different pair that has the same rounded hundredth but different exact values.
