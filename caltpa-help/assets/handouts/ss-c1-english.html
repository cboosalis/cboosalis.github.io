Goals and learning sequence

Content goal. Determine at least two central ideas in the passage, explain how details in different paragraphs develop each idea, and produce an objective summary that represents both.

ALD goal. In discussion and writing, connect a central-idea claim to text evidence with an explicit explanation, using relevant terms such as central idea, detail, develops, and objective, and relationships such as because, although, or therefore when appropriate.

Whole-class orientation and model

Read the goal; distinguish topic, central idea, supporting detail, and objective summary. Briefly clarify reusable and unavailable. Model the thinking process on a separate short example or one limited relationship without supplying both final claims.

Record and collect. Capture the teacher's explanation and students' interpretation checks; avoid a teacher-only clip as evidence of student learning.

Independent first reading

Each learner marks candidate ideas and selects details from different paragraphs. Keep a dated initial response before partner influence.

Record and collect. Keep the task and permitted support conditions inspectable; collect the paper/digital response.

Paired evidence discussion

Each partner proposes two ideas and traces their development; the listener paraphrases, asks which detail supports the reasoning, and challenges an overbroad claim respectfully.

Record and collect. Capture audible justification, listening/paraphrase, the teacher's probe, and any instructional response.

Whole-group comparison and feedback

Compare two plausible interpretations and test each against the full passage. Ask whether maintenance complicates or undermines access, requiring evidence for the answer.

Record and collect. Capture a genuine student interpretation, how the teacher responds, and the subsequent reasoning without predetermining a successful response.

Independent objective summary and metacognition

Write an objective summary representing both ideas and separately explain one evidence-to-idea connection. Identify a detail that caused revision and what still needs checking.

Record and collect. Preserve individual work and support notes; final analysis needs actual products, not participation alone.

Planning an equipment-lending library

Original illustrative informational practice passage created for this companion. It describes a hypothetical proposal, not a report about an actual school or observed results. It is not the unnamed Collections article in the historical sample.

Paragraph 1. A school equipment-lending library would let students borrow items for projects and activities without buying each item themselves. A student might need a drawing kit for one assignment or a ball for a weekend game. Purchasing something for a short period can be difficult for a family. A shared collection could provide another route to participation.

Paragraph 2. Before opening a library, planners would need to find out which items students want to borrow and which are suitable for shared use. A request form could ask about materials without asking families to disclose their incomes. Planners could compare requests with the space, money, and adult supervision available. These choices would shape the first collection.

Paragraph 3. Each reusable item could serve different borrowers at different times. A catalog would show what is available, when an item is due back, and whether another person has requested it. Sharing would not mean that every request could be met immediately. A waiting list would make limited supplies visible and help borrowers plan ahead.

Paragraph 4. A collection alone would not create dependable access. The lending system would also need clear responsibilities. At checkout, a borrower and a staff member could check an item's condition and record a return date. Directions would explain safe use and whom to contact if a part became damaged or went missing. Borrowers would need a way to ask questions.

Paragraph 5. Returns would require attention as well. Staff would inspect equipment before lending it again. An item needing repair would be marked unavailable, even if someone was waiting for it. That delay could be frustrating, but sending out damaged equipment would not provide reliable access. The catalog would need to distinguish items on loan from items awaiting repair.

Paragraph 6. Planners would also need a workable maintenance budget and time for the adults responsible for the collection. Donations might help build the library, but a donated item could bring replacement or repair costs. Clear decisions about these costs would be necessary before adding equipment. A lending rule that no one could maintain would offer little practical support.

Paragraph 7. The proposal therefore connects shared resources with a system for caring for them. To review the design, planners could examine requests, waiting times, and the number of items ready for use, then revise the collection or procedures. These records would help test whether the proposed library was providing dependable opportunities to borrow. They would be questions for future investigation, not results already established.

Brief explicit vocabulary and word-part support

Brief vocabulary access within the central-idea lesson, not a new MS-style foundational-reading requirement.

  • reuse. re- + use: use again. Read the whole word, then confirm its meaning in the equipment context.
  • reusable. reuse + -able: able to be used again; the final silent e in reuse is dropped before -able. Show the spelling explicitly rather than suggesting that word parts always join without changes.
  • unavailable. un- + available: not available. Knowing what available means is necessary before the prefix analysis is useful.
  • responsibility. Teach the whole word in context as a duty or obligation in the proposed system. Do not invent a transparent response + ability split.

Do not cut every word beginning re into re- plus a known base. The spelling of a prefix-like sequence is not sufficient evidence of its meaning.

A bound root and related words: in inspect (paragraph 5), spect is a Latin-derived root form associated with looking; it is not a standalone English word. Read inspect as a whole word and explain “look closely to check.” Compare inspect (action), inspection (the checking process), and inspector (a person who checks). Show inspect + -ion → inspection and inspect + -or → inspector; read and listen to the whole related words, including the pronunciation change in inspection. Confirm meaning in context rather than treating roots as a guessing shortcut. The dictionary word history links this family to Latin forms meaning to look.

Model one use, let partners read and explain a different related word, and collect the short independent practice below. This supports access to the informational text; it does not assume every Grade 7 learner needs a decoding intervention or replace the central-idea goal.

Student word-part reading and writing practice

Read and learn: re- + use → reuse means use again; reuse + -able → reusable means able to be used again (drop the final silent e). un- + available → unavailable means not available. In inspect, the bound root form spect is associated with looking. Read the whole word, then compare inspect, inspection, and inspector. The endings help distinguish an action, a checking process, and a person.

  1. Read these three words aloud or through your approved communication method: inspect, inspection, inspector. Explain the shared meaning and how the words differ. Listen to a partner's explanation and paraphrase it accurately.
  2. Choose a word from that family for each blank and explain your choice: “Before another borrower takes the kit, staff will ___ it.” “The ___ records a missing part.” “The ___ may reveal a missing part.”
  3. Read this hypothetical practice statement: “A park team plans to inspect a reusable ramp. It will remain unavailable until the inspection is complete.” Explain why reusable and unavailable do not contradict each other.
  4. Independently write a new sentence about checking a different shared item. Use inspector and either inspect or inspection accurately. Underline the related forms and explain what each contributes to your sentence.
  5. Return to paragraph 5 of the main passage. Explain what the word inspect helps you understand. Use the paragraph, not only the word parts.

This is practice, not a completed response. Word parts are clues to check against the whole word and context; not every similar letter sequence has the same meaning.

Teacher interpretation key

Teacher interpretation only; not a student's response or evidence of learning.

Topic. A proposed school equipment-lending library.

Central idea 1

Shared equipment can broaden access to activities without requiring each borrower to buy every item.

  • Paragraph 1: Establishes the short-term purchasing barrier and proposes sharing.
  • Paragraph 2: Explains how requests and resources would shape access.
  • Paragraph 3: Explains reuse and the limits of shared availability.
  • Paragraph 7: Proposes evidence for reviewing access rather than asserting success.

Central idea 2

Dependable shared access requires clear responsibilities, care, and resources to maintain the lending system.

  • Paragraph 4: Introduces checkout responsibilities and safe-use communication.
  • Paragraph 5: Shows why inspection and repair can temporarily limit availability.
  • Paragraph 6: Adds the need for sustainable time and funding.
  • Paragraph 7: Connects the system to ongoing review and revision.

Model explanation. Paragraph 5 develops the second idea by showing why an unavailable item may be evidence of necessary maintenance rather than a rule against sharing. It qualifies the first idea: shared ownership can expand opportunities, but dependable access does not mean every item is always immediately available.

Objective summary model. The passage proposes an equipment-lending library as a way to share materials that students might need only temporarily. Requests and a catalog would guide the collection and make limits on availability clear. Reliable borrowing would also require checkout responsibilities, inspection, repair, and resources for maintenance. Planners would review future records to decide whether the design was providing dependable access.

Other defensible formulations are possible if they identify at least two substantive ideas, explain development across the text, and remain accurate and objective.

  • Equipment is only a topic, not a central idea.
  • Every student received everything they needed invents an outcome and contradicts the proposal's limits.
  • Equipment libraries are the best programs is an evaluation, not an objective summary.
  • Listing two unrelated details does not identify two central ideas.

Word-practice key

The blanks are inspect, inspector, and inspection. The transfer statement distinguishes an item's capacity for repeated use from its current availability while checking occurs. Accept a new sentence that uses the chosen word forms accurately and explains their functions; do not treat the teacher key as a learner response.

A reciprocal discussion that makes learning visible

Prepare individually. Each learner records two tentative ideas and paragraph references before talking. A shared answer cannot replace this initial record.

  1. First reader explains. State one proposed idea, name evidence from more than one point in the text, and explain what the later paragraph adds.
  2. Listener paraphrases. Begin with the idea as you understood it. The speaker confirms or corrects that interpretation before the listener responds.
  3. Listener questions the reasoning. Ask which detail supports the relationship or whether another paragraph complicates it. Do not merely ask whether the partner agrees.
  4. Reader responds and revises. Use a paragraph reference and explain whether the question changed the claim, evidence choice, or explanation. Keeping the original idea is acceptable if the reason is supported.
  5. Switch roles. Repeat for the other reader and the second idea. Each learner must explain and listen.
  6. Return to individual work. Each learner writes a summary and one development explanation. Record any partner or teacher assistance rather than presenting a joint answer as independent.

For a group of four, work in two reciprocal pairs, then compare the two interpretations across the table. Roles distribute the intellectual work; they are not fixed labels of ability.

Proposed teacher language and responses

Clarify the demand. “The topic names what the text is about. A central idea states something the text develops about that topic. We need at least two ideas, and we need to explain their development.”

Keep the reasoning with the learner. “You selected paragraph 5. What does that paragraph add to the idea, and how is that different from the information in paragraph 4?”

Probe a tension. “The proposal aims to expand access, but sometimes equipment would be unavailable. How can both statements fit this text?”

Respond conditionally. If a learner supplies only “equipment,” ask what the author says about equipment. If the learner gives two accurate claims without development, compare an early and later paragraph. If the learner adds a personal evaluation to the summary, separate the author's proposal from the reader's judgment. These are proposed moves, not events or student responses reported as having occurred.

Printable student task and evidence map

Text: Planning an equipment-lending library, paragraphs 1–7. Read the entire passage. Use paragraph numbers so another reader can check your reasoning.

  1. State two different central ideas developed in this text. Do more than name the topic.
  2. For each idea, select an earlier and a later paragraph. Explain what the later paragraph adds, develops, or qualifies.
  3. Discuss your reasoning with a partner. Paraphrase the partner's idea before asking a question about its evidence. Record one question that made you check your thinking.
  4. Independently write an objective summary in approximately three or four sentences. Include both ideas; use your own wording; do not add a personal judgment or a result the proposal has not established.

Metacognition before during and after reading

Before: “What does the task require beyond finding the topic? What will count as evidence for two ideas? Which strategy will I try first?”

During: “Does this detail support my claim, or did I choose it because it contains the same word? What did the later paragraph add? Did I understand my partner well enough to paraphrase?”

After: “Which piece of evidence changed or confirmed my explanation? Can I summarize both ideas without adding my preference? What could I do on a new text without this organizer, and what support would I still need?”

Ask learners to point to a decision or revision. Saying “I used a strategy” is not enough to show monitoring or regulation of understanding.

Test the actual room, board or text display, and student voices in advance. A stable oblique view of the teaching area and a consenting discussion group is one possible arrangement, not a mandated camera angle.

Reflect on actual class and focus-student evidence

Use the real recording, individual maps/summaries, and observed discussion process to analyze the group and each required focus student. A useful chain is actual response → interpretation of content and language → teacher decision → subsequent response and remaining uncertainty.

If the work shows two accurate ideas but a detail list instead of development, identify that difference rather than saying the learner “did not understand.” If the oral explanation is sound but the writing is unclear, distinguish a language-expression need from a comprehension gap. If the teacher supplied the relationship, record the support; a repeated answer is not independent transfer.

For each focus student, connect the actual adaptation to the actual response and assess its contribution and limits. Refer to accurate work locations and verified clip spans. No results, quotes, scores, or focus-student profiles are supplied in this companion.

Use evidence to propose the next instruction

Cycle 1 Part H asks for proposed next instruction. It is not the Cycle 2 requirement to teach and record a separate follow-up. Connect the proposal to what the actual group and FS1–FS3 demonstrated, not to a predetermined success story.

If development is the gap: compare a detail list with an explanation of what a later paragraph adds; then use a new short informational passage for an individual relationship explanation. If authoring the summary is the gap: preserve accurate ideas, orally rehearse the relationship, clarify referents/connectives, and collect a new independent summary. If the goals are already met: extend by examining a qualification or interaction among ideas and defending it with evidence from multiple text locations.

Individualize the next move for each actual focus student using the demonstrated asset, need, support conditions, and lesson evidence. Do not simply repeat the same recommendation three times or prescribe a support because of a demographic label.
