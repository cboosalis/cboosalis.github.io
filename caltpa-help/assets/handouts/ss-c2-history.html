Why this is a unit rather than three activities

Proposed teaching design—not a completed CalTPA submission. Tasks, teacher keys, likely interpretation branches and recording plans are examples. No class size, learner profile, assessment result, teaching video, score or learning outcome has been supplied. Replace planning assumptions with your actual placement evidence. The lesson minute ranges are proposed pacing, never video timestamps.

The inquiry is: How should a museum explain attempts to regulate industrial child labor without claiming more than the sources show? Lesson 1 teaches what an enforcement table records. Lesson 2 compares that record with a statute from another country and time. Lesson 3 builds and tests an argument that acknowledges the comparison’s limits. Collaboration helps learners improve reasoning; the final individual response shows what each can do independently. This unit does not use the Cycle 1 report as its only new lesson or pretend that 1862 Britain and 1916 United States form one controlled before/after study.

Align the content and language work

Content goal: distinguish a rule, a documented enforcement action and an outcome claim while constructing a bounded cross-source argument. ELD goal: independently write an attributed argument with because, whereas and although or equivalent relationships, qualifying the reach of its claims. Illustrative references: ELD.PI.9–10.1.Ex, 6b.Ex and 11a/b.Ex; adjust the continuum level using actual evidence, not a fictional profile.

The unit develops the industrial-labor portion of HSS 10.3.4. It does not claim to cover slavery, migration, unions and every country in the overarching standard in three lessons. RH.9–10.1 and WHST.9–10.1 make the reading and writing demands visible, while the history standard keeps the task anchored in historical understanding.

Plan from actual prior assessment

Use a short prior-source analysis to see whether learners can identify source/date, separate a rule from an observation, and cite a detail without overgeneralizing. Obtain a brief spoken explanation as well as writing. A student who can explain the limit orally but loses it in a complex sentence needs a different support from a student who treats all official documents as complete outcome evidence. Use the resulting actual examples to choose the focus student and adapt the unit. Invite relevant interests in archives, public policy, work, technology or community history without requiring personal family disclosures.

Learner packet: source set and inquiry tools

Teacher-written context: Industrialization changed how labor was organized and intensified public debate about working conditions. In Britain, the 1833 Factory Act restricted some child factory labor and established an inspectorate. A law states what is required; it does not by itself show compliance. This lesson examines what a later inspector’s account can and cannot establish. It does not ask students to role-play exploited children, defend abuse or disclose family work experiences. Discuss historical evidence with care and allow a private way to ask for support.

Source C: Keating-Owen Child Labor Act, U.S. Congress, approved September 1, 1916, section 3 (excerpt). The full act sought to restrict interstate and foreign shipment of goods produced under specified child-labor conditions. Read the enforcement language below; do not treat it as a report that inspections occurred.

That for the purpose of securing proper enforcement of this Act the Secretary of Labor, or any person duly authorized by him, shall have authority to enter and inspect at any time mines quarries, mills, canneries, workshops, factories, manufacturing establishments, and other places in which goods are produced or held for interstate commerce; and the Secretary of Labor shall have authority to employ such assistance for the purposes of this Act as may from time to time be authorized by appropriation or other law.

Source D: modern archival context, teacher paraphrase. The U.S. National Archives explains that the Supreme Court invalidated this act in Hammer v. Dagenhart in 1918. That later event matters when evaluating a claim that passing the 1916 act permanently ended child labor. This contextual statement is from a modern secondary account, not part of the quoted statute. The 1916 law is historical, not current legal guidance.

A protocol for genuine disciplinary collaboration

Collaboration is an intellectual routine, not simply seating students together. Each student first makes a private attempt. In pairs, the explainer states a claim and points to evidence; the listener paraphrases it before asking an evidence question. The original speaker confirms or corrects the paraphrase. Partners switch roles, negotiate a revision, and record what changed and why. Everyone then completes a new individual response. Do not assign the same student permanently to note-taking or translating; both students need opportunities to read, speak, listen, reason and write.

Observable collaboration—not a participation tally

The language target is a disciplinary action, not a vocabulary count. Teach how to link a claim to evidence and qualify a conclusion. Model one short example, unpack the language, give guided practice, then remove unnecessary scaffolds. Let students draw, gesture, annotate and rehearse using their language resources before producing the required English response. Do not turn the conference into a debate over whether children deserved protection. The evidence question is what the documents allow historians to conclude. Independent summative task—student copy

Student task: A museum draft says, “These records prove that governments ended industrial child labor once they passed laws.” Write a 200–300 word evidence-based replacement, or an agreed concise equivalent that preserves the same reasoning demand. Use Source B and Source C, with Source D for later context. State a qualified claim, use at least two precise details, explain what each contributes, address an alternative reading of the enforcement record, and identify one missing kind of evidence. Give dates/countries so the reader cannot mistake the sources for one before/after study. Include a short source list.

Teacher interpretation key—not student work

Teacher interpretation key: A defensible argument says the records show attempts to regulate industrial child labor and some recorded enforcement activity, while leaving important effects uncertain. Source B records offences, penalties and withdrawn cases; it cannot supply a national success rate without information about all relevant workplaces/cases. Source C authorizes inspection under the 1916 law; it is not a record of inspections actually performed. Source D states the law was invalidated in 1918. It prevents the draft’s permanent-end claim but does not measure all child labor. More recorded cases can indicate persistent violations, active enforcement, or both; the selected table cannot settle their balance. Different countries/dates do not constitute a controlled trend. Accept alternative carefully supported arguments, not only this wording.

Analyze actual assessment results against content and ELD targets separately. Include denominators and missing work; do not fill a “met” table with imagined counts. Keep the focus student’s independent argument, actual feedback and subsequent revision so later claims can be checked.

Conditional re-teach: a new historical detail

Use only if actual focus-student evidence identifies a rule/action/outcome confusion. Read a previously unused entry from the same archived report. The 1 October 1862 row names John Howell, Mynyddislwyn, records four young persons employed after 2 p.m. on Saturday, enters a £1 penalty and notes three cases withdrawn on payment of costs. This is a teacher-organized, shortened selection of an actual row—not invented testimony. Compare it with section 3 of the 1916 U.S. act already read. Which source grants authority, and which records a case? Write an attributed contrast and explain why neither alone proves that all factories complied. Identify one further record you would seek.

First, the teacher may model the distinction using a different already-read row. For this new check, the learner explains the John Howell row independently and then checks the written contrast against the source.

Re-teach teacher key and interpretation

The statute grants inspection authority; the 1862 row records an offence/case with a penalty and specified withdrawals. The row is evidence of this recorded proceeding, not all workplaces’ compliance; the statute does not document that an inspection occurred. A further useful source might be an inspection register including compliant workplaces, the case file or follow-up enforcement records. Students should preserve the different countries/dates. If the distinction is correct orally but attribution is lost in writing, target the language rather than reteach the historical concept wholesale.

Conditional extension: choose evidence that can test a claim

Use only if actual evidence supports extension. A historian wants to test the claim “Enforcement became more consistent in this British region over time.” Three proposed search targets are available: (A) another printed copy of the same law, (B) a regional inspection register with dated visits, including visits where no offence was found, across several years, or (C) a later reformer’s opinion article. These are proposed search targets, not documents claimed to have been found. Which would most directly help test the claim, and why? Explain what denominator and contextual information you would still need. State one way another target could be useful for a different historical question. Discuss a partner’s challenge before writing your own qualified recommendation.

Extension teacher key and interpretation

B most directly offers information about recorded inspections and their outcomes over time, potentially including a denominator of visits. It still does not automatically represent every workplace: coverage, selection, changing rules, repeat visits and recording practices matter. A law copy helps establish legal wording, not enforcement frequency. An opinion article can illuminate arguments or perspectives, but it is not by itself a representative enforcement measure. Accept a different choice only when the learner changes or sharply qualifies the question and justifies the fit. A useful extension evaluates source function and representativeness, not merely adds another quotation.

Record the key learning decisions

Before teaching, follow your school and program’s consent procedures, identify who may appear, and store recordings only in approved locations. Test the actual device in the room: battery, storage, focus, lighting and whether both teacher and student voices are intelligible. Put a stable camera at a side/back angle that includes the teaching area and a consented discussion table without blocking movement. Avoid filming unconsented learners, displayed names, gradebooks or private records. A teacher-only microphone may lose the student thinking you need; test a permitted table microphone or a closer camera position during ordinary discussion.

Plan where students will work before the recording begins, but do not stage answers or rehearse an assessed performance for the camera. Retain the original securely. Later select authentic moments and follow the current guide’s editing and technical rules and log clip number, exact start/end, prompt, visible action, student response and limitation. A seat map does not replace audible evidence. Photograph or scan work separately when the camera cannot show writing legibly.

When something goes wrong, respond to the learning and then analyze it honestly. An incomplete explanation, disagreement or unsuccessful support can yield useful evidence if you describe what actually happened and why you changed course. Missing sound or an unseen learner cannot be repaired by inventing dialogue. Do not stage a reenactment; ask your program how to handle unusable required evidence. Planning a recording beat is not a guarantee that a usable clip exists.
