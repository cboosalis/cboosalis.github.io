What this example teaches

Proposed teaching design—not a completed CalTPA submission. Tasks, teacher keys, likely interpretation branches and recording plans are examples. No class size, learner profile, assessment result, teaching video, score or learning outcome has been supplied. Replace planning assumptions with your actual placement evidence. The lesson minute ranges are proposed pacing, never video timestamps.

A diagram becomes scientific reasoning when a learner can explain the arrows, use a model to test a claim, and revise the representation. The important distinction is matter versus energy. Naming “photosynthesis” is not yet an explanation. An accurate but hesitant spoken explanation may show more conceptual understanding than a fluent recitation of a memorized equation.

Standards, goals and success criteria

Content goal: construct and justify a photosynthesis model that traces matter, conserves the represented atoms, and distinguishes the transformation of light energy from the movement of matter.

ALD goal: explain matter and energy relationships, listen closely enough to paraphrase, and justify a model revision. Cycle 1 asks for an ALD goal; do not relabel it as a required proficiency score.

Find out what learners know before planning

Use a recent model or short precheck: “Draw what a plant takes in and gives out while growing; label which arrows represent matter and which represent energy.” Ask each learner to explain one arrow in a second mode. Compare the drawing, spoken explanation and reading of the prompt. Record the actual date, support and response. A student who draws carbon entering from air but misuses “food” needs a different response from a student whose model turns sunlight into atoms. Ask about relevant interests such as gardening or food systems without presuming any identity or experience. Learner materials: complete reference and task

Teacher-written science reference card (original wording; not a research report): Green plants use light energy during photosynthesis. Carbon dioxide and water provide matter used to make sugars; oxygen is released. A simplified overall model is 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2, with light energy entering the process. Count carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms on both sides. Atoms are rearranged, not created from sunlight. Sugar is matter that stores chemical energy. A matter arrow and an energy arrow therefore mean different things. This summary omits the many biochemical steps; it is a model for reasoning about inputs, outputs and energy transformation, not an animation of every reaction.

CDE: HS-LS1-5, modeling photosynthesis

  1. Before reading: draw your initial model. Mark one part you are unsure about.
  2. Read the reference card twice: first for the overall process; then underline matter inputs/outputs and circle energy language.
  3. Build a model using labeled carbon, hydrogen and oxygen counters or drawn symbols. Represent six carbon dioxide molecules and six water molecules on the input side. Rearrange the represented atoms into one glucose molecule and six oxygen molecules. Count each element before and after.
  4. Add an arrow for light energy and explain why it is not an extra atom. Label what the model omits.
  5. Exchange explanations using the collaboration protocol. Ask: “Where is the carbon in the sugar coming from?” and “Does your arrow show a substance, energy, or both without distinction?”
  6. Revise one feature, or defend keeping it, with a scientific reason. Independently explain why “the plant turns sunlight into new matter” is not an adequate description.

Accessible materials: large-print reference card, a high-contrast diagram, manipulatives with both shapes and letters (not color alone), bilingual glossary if useful, blank paper and an optional sentence support: “The ___ enters as matter; ___ provides energy. I know these are different because ___.” The frame does not provide the missing scientific relationship.

Keep the standard in view: Atom counting is a support for tracing matter, not the endpoint of HS-LS1-5. A balanced count without an explanation of the matter inputs/outputs and transformation of light energy does not meet this lesson’s modeling goal. Do not let a lengthy symbolic calculation displace the scientific explanation.

Make listening, speaking and revision observable

Collaboration is an intellectual routine, not simply seating students together. Each student first makes a private attempt. In pairs, the explainer states a claim and points to evidence; the listener paraphrases it before asking an evidence question. The original speaker confirms or corrects the paraphrase. Partners switch roles, negotiate a revision, and record what changed and why. Everyone then completes a new individual response. Do not assign the same student permanently to note-taking or translating; both students need opportunities to read, speak, listen, reason and write.

Observable collaboration—not a participation tally
MoveWhat a teacher can look for

Listen accuratelyThe listener restates the claim and evidence in a way the speaker accepts or corrects.
Challenge respectfullyThe partner asks what a line, number or arrow supports and what it does not establish.
Revise with a reasonThe learner names the evidence that changed or strengthened the reasoning. Agreement is not required.
Transfer independentlyA new response shows what the individual can do after support; a strong pair product alone is insufficient.

Teach the language of a scientific model

The language target is a disciplinary action, not a vocabulary count. Teach how to link a claim to evidence and qualify a conclusion. Model one short example, unpack the language, give guided practice, then remove unnecessary scaffolds. Let students draw, gesture, annotate and rehearse using their language resources before producing the required English response. Unpack a sentence such as “Carbon dioxide provides carbon atoms for sugar, whereas light supplies energy.” Contrast provides atoms with supplies energy and explain the relationship expressed by whereas. Invite students to point while speaking, then write an explanation without relying on the partner’s wording. Treat “food” carefully: plants synthesize sugars; soil minerals are important but are not interchangeable with sugar or the carbon source in this model.

Independent exit task—student copy

Student exit task: A classmate claims, “The plant gets its new sugar from soil, and light becomes the sugar’s atoms.” Draw a corrected model and write 3–5 sentences explaining the source of the carbon and the role of light. Include one conserved atom count. Finish: “The idea I changed or checked was ___; the evidence or model feature that helped was ___; I still need to check ___.”

Teacher interpretation key—not student work

Teacher interpretation key—not student work: Carbon in sugar is traced to CO2; water supplies hydrogen in the simplified model; glucose and oxygen are outputs. Six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and eighteen oxygen atoms occur on each side of the displayed overall equation. Light energy is transformed into stored chemical energy; it is not the source of carbon atoms. Accept different clear models and wording. An unlabeled energy arrow warrants a follow-up question, not an automatic judgment that the learner lacks the concept.

Plan usable evidence, including imperfect teaching

Before teaching, follow your school and program’s consent procedures, identify who may appear, and store recordings only in approved locations. Test the actual device in the room: battery, storage, focus, lighting and whether both teacher and student voices are intelligible. Put a stable camera at a side/back angle that includes the teaching area and a consented discussion table without blocking movement. Avoid filming unconsented learners, displayed names, gradebooks or private records. A teacher-only microphone may lose the student thinking you need; test a permitted table microphone or a closer camera position during ordinary discussion.

Plan where students will work before the recording begins, but do not stage answers or rehearse an assessed performance for the camera. Retain the original securely. Later select authentic moments and follow the current guide’s editing and technical rules and log clip number, exact start/end, prompt, visible action, student response and limitation. A seat map does not replace audible evidence. Photograph or scan work separately when the camera cannot show writing legibly.

When something goes wrong, respond to the learning and then analyze it honestly. An incomplete explanation, disagreement or unsuccessful support can yield useful evidence if you describe what actually happened and why you changed course. Missing sound or an unseen learner cannot be repaired by inventing dialogue. Do not stage a reenactment; ask your program how to handle unusable required evidence. Planning a recording beat is not a guarantee that a usable clip exists.

Important recording beat: Capture the transition from a student’s initial claim to a justified revision, not only your polished explanation. Keep the initial and final individual model, with actual support conditions, so the commentary can distinguish supported participation from independent reasoning.
