The unit’s learning progression

Proposed teaching design—not a completed CalTPA submission. Tasks, teacher keys, likely interpretation branches and recording plans are examples. No class size, learner profile, assessment result, teaching video, score or learning outcome has been supplied. Replace planning assumptions with your actual placement evidence. The lesson minute ranges are proposed pacing, never video timestamps.

Lesson 1 establishes photosynthesis as a matter-and-energy model. Lesson 2 adds respiration and the distinction between a process and its net measurement. Lesson 3 asks learners to integrate the models, critique an explanation and transfer independently. The follow-up is selected from the focus student’s actual evidence; it is not a prewritten fourth lesson presented as if a need had been observed.

Content, ELD and assessment alignment

Unit content goal: connect photosynthesis and respiration in a defensible model that explains net oxygen change and distinguishes matter from energy. Unit ELD goal: independently write a coherent causal explanation from a diagram and table, using contrast and qualification to show what the evidence permits. Illustrative anchors: ELD.PI.9–10.1.Ex and 6b.Ex; select the real learner’s level from the full continuum. Each of the three lessons below includes an ELD goal, even when a group has no identified English learner.

Assess content and language separately. A correct process model with unclear linking language needs different feedback from an eloquent explanation with oxygen used as an energy synonym. The individual summative task measures both goals; observations of collaborative listening complement the written product rather than being inferred from it.

Learner packet: two reference cards and a simulated dataset

Teacher-written science reference card (original wording; not a research report): Green plants use light energy during photosynthesis. Carbon dioxide and water provide matter used to make sugars; oxygen is released. A simplified overall model is 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2, with light energy entering the process. Count carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms on both sides. Atoms are rearranged, not created from sunlight. Sugar is matter that stores chemical energy. A matter arrow and an energy arrow therefore mean different things. This summary omits the many biochemical steps; it is a model for reasoning about inputs, outputs and energy transformation, not an animation of every reaction.

CDE: HS-LS1-5, modeling photosynthesis

Teacher-written companion reference card (original wording): Plants also carry out cellular respiration. A simplified overall model uses sugar and oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water. Chemical bonds are rearranged, with a net transfer of energy that can support cellular processes and warm the surroundings. Breaking a chemical bond requires energy; the overall reaction can transfer energy because new bonds form as well. Do not explain respiration as energy being created or as bond breaking alone releasing energy. Photosynthesis needs light; respiration can occur in light and darkness. An oxygen reading from a plant system can show the net result of more than one process, not a direct measurement of photosynthesis by itself.

CDE: HS-LS1-7, modeling cellular respiration

Keep intellectual responsibility shared—and individual

Collaboration is an intellectual routine, not simply seating students together. Each student first makes a private attempt. In pairs, the explainer states a claim and points to evidence; the listener paraphrases it before asking an evidence question. The original speaker confirms or corrects the paraphrase. Partners switch roles, negotiate a revision, and record what changed and why. Everyone then completes a new individual response. Do not assign the same student permanently to note-taking or translating; both students need opportunities to read, speak, listen, reason and write.

Independent summative task—student copy

Student task: A teacher-created follow-on simulation starts all chambers at 8.0 mg/L dissolved oxygen. After 20 minutes, the plant/light chamber is 8.5, the plant/dark chamber is 7.7, and both no-plant chambers remain 8.0. These are invented instructional numbers, not a second real experiment. Draw a connected model of photosynthesis and respiration; show matter inputs/outputs and energy transfers. Write an explanation that (1) calculates the two plant changes, (2) explains why a positive light result does not show respiration has stopped, (3) uses the no-plant comparison appropriately, and (4) states one limitation. Then independently respond to “Plants only respire at night.”

Teacher interpretation key—not student work

Teacher interpretation key: The changes are +0.5 and −0.3 mg/L over 20 minutes. Net oxygen gain can occur while respiration also consumes oxygen if photosynthetic production is larger. The dark result is consistent with oxygen-consuming respiration without light-driven photosynthesis. No-plant stability helps check changes unrelated to the plant under the stated conditions but does not eliminate every confound. The dataset alone does not directly measure gross photosynthesis, cellular energy transfer, general biological rates or statistical reliability. Accept clear alternative diagrams. If scoring numerically for class use, establish and communicate the local rule before assessment; these descriptors are not learner scores and not CalTPA rating predictions.

For the official analysis, report actual counts for content and ELD goals separately, including the denominator and missing/unscorable work. Do not invent a filled table to make the model appear complete. Link a focus-student claim to the exact work, actual feedback and subsequent response.

Conditional re-teach: ready-to-use student check

Use only if actual focus-student evidence identifies a net-change misconception. This is an original conceptual simulation, not laboratory data. In one interval, a lighted plant model shows 7 arbitrary oxygen units produced by photosynthesis and 4 used by respiration. (1) Show both process arrows and calculate the net change. (2) Explain why a positive change does not mean respiration stopped. (3) A different interval has a net change of −2 units. Give two possible production/consumption pairs with that net change, and explain why net change alone does not identify both process amounts. Use a causal connection and one limitation in your explanation.

Before the new check, a teacher may model a different pair of amounts, ask the learner to point to the two processes and hear a paraphrase. Complete the 7/4 and −2 tasks independently afterward, with required accommodations. Explain which support, if any, helped you distinguish a process from a net measurement.

Re-teach teacher key and interpretation

For the new check, 7−4=+3 units. Both process arrows remain: production exceeds consumption, but consumption is not zero. For net −2, possible pairs include 2 produced/4 consumed and 4 produced/6 consumed. Many pairs fit; the net alone cannot recover both amounts. These arbitrary model units are not measured mg/L, rates or student results. If the arithmetic is wrong but both process relationships are clear, ask a brief calculation question before diagnosing a biological misconception. Check the causal explanation separately from the content model.

Conditional extension: ready-to-use student inquiry

Use only if actual content and ELD evidence supports extension. Evaluate two proposed investigations; neither is an experiment reported as completed. Plan A compares one plant chamber in light at 22°C with one in darkness at 15°C. Plan B compares repeated matched plant chambers at the same temperature, water volume and biomass, varying light, with matched no-plant comparisons and a checked oxygen sensor. Which design better tests a light-related difference, and why? Identify one remaining inference limit, propose an additional measurement or control, and explain how it could test a claim. Paraphrase a partner’s design critique before writing your independent recommendation.

Extension teacher key and interpretation

Plan A changes temperature with light and uses one chamber per condition, so a difference is confounded and variability is unexamined. Plan B improves control and repetition; it still measures net oxygen, not each gross process directly, and its results would remain tied to its organisms/conditions. A defensible proposal names the uncertainty it addresses, such as monitoring temperature, calibrating/checking sensors or collecting an additional process-relevant measure. Do not require a single preselected design addition. Evaluate the reason and appropriately qualified language, not the number of scientific terms.

Plan video and work evidence across the unit

Before teaching, follow your school and program’s consent procedures, identify who may appear, and store recordings only in approved locations. Test the actual device in the room: battery, storage, focus, lighting and whether both teacher and student voices are intelligible. Put a stable camera at a side/back angle that includes the teaching area and a consented discussion table without blocking movement. Avoid filming unconsented learners, displayed names, gradebooks or private records. A teacher-only microphone may lose the student thinking you need; test a permitted table microphone or a closer camera position during ordinary discussion.

Plan where students will work before the recording begins, but do not stage answers or rehearse an assessed performance for the camera. Retain the original securely. Later select authentic moments and follow the current guide’s editing and technical rules and log clip number, exact start/end, prompt, visible action, student response and limitation. A seat map does not replace audible evidence. Photograph or scan work separately when the camera cannot show writing legibly.

When something goes wrong, respond to the learning and then analyze it honestly. An incomplete explanation, disagreement or unsuccessful support can yield useful evidence if you describe what actually happened and why you changed course. Missing sound or an unseen learner cannot be repaired by inventing dialogue. Do not stage a reenactment; ask your program how to handle unusable required evidence. Planning a recording beat is not a guarantee that a usable clip exists.
